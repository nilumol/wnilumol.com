Halsey: my AI chief of staff

I have AI agents that do things: one manages my software projects, another scans job boards, another is meant to coach me through a certification. What I didn't have was someone keeping the whole picture: what I'm trying to do, whether I'm on track, what changed today, and what I should do about it. So I designed one. Its name is Halsey.

What Halsey is

Halsey is a chief of staff, and I mean that in the specific sense. A good chief of staff doesn't do the principal's job, and doesn't build the company's products. It keeps the principal's attention on what matters. It tracks commitments, notices when something is slipping, researches before recommending, pushes back when a plan doesn't fit, and hands work to the right people. It also remembers what was decided and why.

That's the job I designed for Halsey. Today I can talk with it and set it up. The fuller design calls for it to research, judge, strategize, summarize, record my data, and analyze whether I'm on track. It is meant to keep working to understand what I'm actually after: my longer-term goals, the milestones on the way, and the values behind them.

Halsey was inspired by Firstmate, the agent that already manages my software projects. In the design, Halsey hands anything that needs building to that agent rather than writing software itself.

That last point is a deliberate line, not a limitation. The plan is for Halsey to make things I read: a daily page, a weekly review, reports, spreadsheets, charts, and briefings, all generated from its own data and fixed templates. When its own analysis needs a quick calculation, it may write and run a small throwaway script in a sandbox. But anything that has to be kept, runs on a schedule, is depended on by something else, or connects to something new goes to the builder. There, it gets built properly, reviewed automatically, and checked by Halsey against what I actually asked for before I ever see it.

Three rules guide the design:

  • Every number says where it came from. It was counted from an agent's records, I told Halsey in a check-in, or it's a flagged estimate. Time spent is never assumed to be measured, and a number nobody reported shows as "not reported", never as zero.
  • Halsey asks; it never overrules. It can propose a change to my goals, challenge a plan, or question whether a goal still fits me. Nothing about my goals, values, or its own rules changes without my explicit Accept.
  • Nobody edits anyone else's files. Agents send Halsey reports, and Halsey sends them requests. That's the whole interface.

How it's organized

The design has four layers, and the boundaries between them matter more than anything inside them.

The planned contract is a small, versioned file format I call an Area Report. Each agent would write one for its area of my life every period. It would hold the numbers tied to my goals, each with its source; what happened, how, and with what results; anything waiting on me; the questions it needs me to answer, because its records can't; and the changes it would suggest. Going the other way, an Area Request would ask an agent to change how it works.

The format only grows by adding optional fields, so an old report keeps working when the format gains something new. A report that fails validation is set aside with its reason, never half-read.

Keeping the interface this narrow is what lets new agents plug in later. A health agent, a study coach, or an agent living inside my spreadsheets would each just be one more thing that files a report.

The daily page

The planned daily page will show everything Halsey knows about my day. It will open from an icon and rebuild from the underlying records each time I open it, with a design target of about a tenth of a second to open. It is meant to avoid a dashboard that went stale overnight.

From top to bottom:

  • Today: the four check-ins with their status, and a line saying what changed since the last one.
  • Anything waiting from a skipped check-in: if I declined the morning check-in, its number questions wait here as one-tap answers, with a button that opens the conversation if I want it after all.
  • A short summary. The design target is for the counts to appear immediately and a few sentences from the model to follow within a few seconds. If the model is unavailable, I still get the counts.
  • My list, which is a plain text file I can also edit anywhere else.
  • What other agents are waiting on me for. My project agent alone often has more than twenty open questions for me. A chief of staff's most underrated job is gatekeeping. Only three get through each day, picked by a rule I set: whatever blocks a goal number first, then the oldest. The rest are listed as "can wait", never hidden.
  • Builds that are ready for my look, meaning only the ones that already passed Halsey's review.
  • My goals with their numbers, each with its source and a small alignment tag.

When I answer one of those waiting questions from the page, my exact words are sent to the agent that asked. Halsey never paraphrases an answer on my behalf, and it never answers a decision in words I didn't write.

Check-ins: four short conversations a day

The numbers that matter most are often the ones no system records: how long I actually spent on the job search, whether I studied, what came up in a conversation. My first design asked for them with a one-tap prompt every morning. That was efficient and shallow: three quick answers, and nothing that could get to why something was stuck.

So the planned check-ins are conversations. The design calls for four a day:

9:00 is the main check-in: the target is five to ten minutes. Halsey collects last night's numbers if I missed the evening one, confirms my top priorities for the day, asks anything an agent needs to know (did I hear back about that application?), and picks one topic to dig into. That might be a goal that's slipping, a decision I keep putting off, or a question about whether a plan still fits what I care about.

13:00 and 17:00 are quick: "Any big status updates, or anything new come up?" If the answer is no, the target is to finish in twenty seconds. If something changed, Halsey asks what, and adjusts the day's plan.

21:00 is the wrap-up: today's numbers while they're fresh, what got done against the morning's plan, what carries over, and a draft of tomorrow's top three for the morning check-in to confirm.

A doorbell, not a form

A pop-up can't hold a conversation, so the pop-up is just a doorbell. At each time, a small window appears with two buttons: Check in with Halsey and Decline. Check in opens a terminal window where Halsey is already talking. Decline records the check-in as skipped, with no second ring and no nagging.

Inside, Halsey asks at most five questions, and follow-ups count. The cap is what keeps "conversational" from turning into "interrogation". Here's the shape of a morning check-in, with the numbers made up:

Halsey Morning. Three things, then I'll let you go. About how long did you spend on the job search yesterday?

Me maybe 45 min. and I applied to one on a company's own site

› saved: 45 min · logged: an application, company website

Halsey Thanks. Replies are running at 3% against a 6% target, and most recent applications weren't tailored. What gets in the way of tailoring?

Me it takes an hour each and I run out of steam after work

Halsey So it's energy at the end of the day, not skill. Would a morning slot twice a week work, or does that cost your study time?

Me mornings are for studying. maybe Saturday

Halsey Then I'll propose tailoring two applications on Saturday mornings, as a two-week experiment watching the reply rate. You'll see it on the daily page to accept. Last one: what are your top three today?

Two design choices matter here. First, every answer is saved the moment I give it, so closing the window halfway keeps what I said. Second, every outcome is recorded honestly. A check-in can be answered, cut short, declined, left unanswered, or missed because the laptop was asleep. Any number that didn't get answered is "not reported" in every chart and total, never zero.

Between check-ins

A chief of staff tracks a changing day, not just four moments in it. Between check-ins, Halsey keeps the daily page current: replies from other agents, a build that's ready for me, research that finished, a new report. A "since your last check-in" line lists what changed. Halsey doesn't interrupt me for any of it. Instead, at the next check-in it raises the one thing that changed the picture, like a new request that blocks a goal, or a plan item that clearly won't fit anymore.

This is the most expensive part of the design, and I want to be honest about it. I estimate four conversations a day would take 10 to 20 minutes, up from an estimated 30 seconds of tapping in my first design. My model-input estimate is roughly half a million to a million tokens a day, most of it repeated context. While designing this, I actually hit my plan's usage limit. Whether the depth is worth that is exactly what the first weeks of use will answer.

The records log: the tracker I don't keep

I don't keep a tracker in a notes app or a spreadsheet. I've tried, and it lasts about a week. The records log is meant to make Halsey the tracker. I would mention things in plain conversation, like "applied to one through a referral" or "had coffee with a former colleague who might refer me", and Halsey would turn them into structured entries in a local database: a category, the fields that category needs, a date, and where the entry came from, including my own words.

It works like this:

  • Plain facts are saved immediately, with a one-line read-back I can correct. "Logged: application · company website · yesterday. Say fix to change it."
  • When Halsey isn't sure something should be stored, it asks. That covers a plan rather than something done, how I felt, details about another person, anything health- or money-related, or anything that doesn't fit a category.
  • It never logs from inference. Only what I said, or what an agent reported, becomes an entry.
  • Corrections are just conversation ("that was Tuesday, not Monday"), and every change is kept in a history. A deleted entry can be restored for 30 days, then it's gone for good.

The design would then let me ask for overviews whenever I want: everything I did this week, or every application with how I applied and what came of it. These would be answered by plain database queries, so the AI would only word the answer. It wouldn't compute it.

The subtle part is counting. Say I mark an application in the job agent's tracker on Monday, then mention the same application in Tuesday's check-in with the detail that it came through a referral. That's one application, not two. Halsey matches entries by a shared identifier when there is one. When it only probably matches (same company, similar role, a day apart), it asks me instead of merging silently. Four applications from the agent plus one I only mentioned makes five, not six.

The log is also not a journal. It holds facts: what I did and what happened. Reflection stays out of it unless I explicitly ask to keep something.

Research, thesis first

An agent that recommends things needs to know things, and "search the web and summarize" is a weak way to know things. It returns whatever ranks well, and a confident summary of SEO content is worse than no answer.

Halsey's planned research workflow runs thesis first, in three moves: think, then check, using trusted sources only.

First, the model forms a thesis from its own reasoning, with web search turned off. Then it breaks the thesis into specific, testable claims. Each claim gets a confidence level, a sentence saying what evidence would prove it wrong, and a flag if it depends on things that change, like a job market, prices, or recent events. For those claims, the model's training data may simply be out of date.

Then each claim is checked separately. It's searched for evidence that supports it and evidence that contradicts it, built from that "what would prove it wrong" sentence. The check only sees the claims, not the persuasive thesis they came from.

What counts as evidence is defined in tiers:

TierWhatCan it verify a claim?
1Government and official statistics, peer-reviewed research, primary company filings, official documentationYes, on its own
2Established industry datasets with a stated method, sample, and dateYes, if recent enough
3Reputable journalism or analysis that cites tier 1 or 2Only two independent ones together
4SEO blogs, vendor marketing, forums, AI-written summariesNever

The tier comes from a list I approve for each topic, not from the model's opinion. Halsey can propose adding a source it keeps running into, but until I accept it, the source can't verify anything.

The planned step I trust most is the dullest one. A plain script, with no AI, will re-check the model's work. It will re-open every cited page and confirm the quoted sentence is actually there. It will read the publication date from the page itself, apply the tier list, and compute verdicts by fixed rules. A claim will be confirmed, contradicted, or unverified. Contradicted claims will be rewritten, and the revised thesis will be what gets saved.

What happened in a manual trial

I tried the thesis-first reasoning and model-led source-checking steps on a question I care about: typical conversion rates through a job-search funnel, and how long a search takes. The research workflow and its script re-check are not built yet. The model's thesis, written without searching, was reasonable-sounding: roughly 3–10% of applications get a screen, 10–25% of interview processes end in an offer, and a search takes 3–6 months.

The check told a more interesting story. One industry dataset put interview-to-offer at 36.2%, up from 16–19% in earlier years. That contradicts the thesis, which then gets revised upward. Most other claims ended up unverified. The public data measured something close but not the same, such as how long people stay unemployed, which isn't how long a search takes, or it didn't separate the stages the claim was about. That's the honest result. The real evidence will be my own numbers.

The model's own check also made exactly the mistakes the planned script is meant to catch:

  • It misquoted a government statistic: 9.3 weeks where the table says 10.3.
  • It marked a claim "confirmed" from two papers it never opened.
  • It called a dated page "undated".

I checked those by opening the pages myself. Catching them by hand is why the planned script re-check matters.

The design still has weaknesses. A model can anchor on its first idea, so the check sees claims rather than the narrative, and ideally a different model does the checking. The best sources are often paywalled or a year or two behind. Some trusted sites block scripted access entirely. When evidence can't be machine-checked, the claim stays unverified, which is the right default.

Strategy for open-ended goals

"Pass the exam by December" is a structured goal: there's a syllabus, a practice score, and a date. "Land a new role in two to four months" is open-ended. Nobody hands you the plan, and working harder at the wrong stage doesn't help.

The planned strategy workflow would give open-ended goals an approach, milestones, and three to five KPIs, with targets that come from checked research, not from vibes. For a job search, those would be stage-by-stage conversion rates: applications to first screens, screens to interviews, interviews to offers. Halsey would track them weekly. When a KPI misses, it would work out which stage is failing and why, using my own data first. Are the roles a good fit? How many applications were tailored? How many came through referrals? Then it would propose options, each labeled by where the evidence comes from: research, Halsey's judgment, or my data.

An approved change runs as a small experiment: one change, one number to watch, and a date to check. If the reply rate is flat after two weeks of tailoring, that's evidence too. What Halsey learns goes into a playbook for that area, with sources and confidence levels that get updated as my own results arrive. That's how Halsey is meant to get genuinely better at helping me over time, not just more verbose.

Goals and values, as a continuous job

A chief of staff that only asks "are you hitting the number?" is a very expensive spreadsheet. The more useful question is whether the number is still the right one.

In the full design, understanding my goals and values isn't a quarterly exercise. It is one of Halsey's standing jobs. Halsey would keep working out my longer-term goals and milestones, and the values behind them, from four sources:

  • the check-in conversations, which are the main source and a big reason check-ins became conversations;
  • the weekly review;
  • the decisions I make, which are recorded in my own words;
  • patterns in the records, like where my time actually goes and which goals keep slipping.

Each goal gets a small alignment signal: fits, tension, conflict, or unclear. Each signal carries the evidence and the question Halsey would ask me. "You turned down the higher-volume plan and said you'd rather send three applications you're proud of. Should I stop proposing volume increases?" is a far better intervention than another reminder to apply to more jobs.

The guardrails haven't moved. Halsey proposes wording for a goal, a milestone, or a value. Only my Accept writes it down. The alignment signal is a question, never a verdict, and Halsey never pauses or reprioritizes a goal on its own. Its observations are kept separately and labeled as its understanding, never presented as my values.

The weekly review

The planned weekly review would give Halsey a fixed way to step back:

  • A scorecard for every goal: each number against its target, with its source and its change since last week.
  • Halsey's read: a short paragraph on what actually moved and why. It's the kind of observation I'd never make myself, like "you're one application short, but the bigger leak is earlier: three good postings closed before you looked at them."
  • Up to three proposed changes, each with a number to watch and a date to check. One of them is allowed to be a challenge to the plan itself.
  • Check-ups on running experiments: keep, change, or drop.
  • What Halsey learned about my goals and values that week, with any proposed wording for me to accept.

Approving a proposal turns it into a request to the right agent, or into a build. Declining it goes into memory, so the same idea doesn't come back next week dressed differently. Once a quarter the review is bigger: goals, milestones, and Halsey's own charter get a refresh, all as proposed changes I accept or don't.

Reports that line up week to week

If a weekly report is written freehand by a model, you can't compare two weeks of it. Sections move, numbers get rounded differently, and every so often a number appears that nobody can trace.

The plan gives every report Halsey makes a fixed, versioned structure. Each type, whether a weekly scorecard, a report, a chart, a dashboard, or a briefing, would have a schema with the same sections and columns every week. Everything would be shaped as tables, so it maps straight onto a spreadsheet sheet or a database table.

  • Numbers come only from Halsey's own records, filled in by named queries. The model never writes a number into a table.
  • The model fills only named text fields, like a 90-word "Halsey's read" or a 40-word "one change to try". A check rejects any number in that text that doesn't match a cell in the tables.
  • The readable versions are rendered from the structured data by templates: the web page, the Markdown, and the Excel file.

The weekly scorecard, for example, is a spreadsheet with a Scorecard sheet (goal, metric, value, target, status, source, change since last week), plus sheets for alignment, running experiments, a summary of the records log, and the notes. The columns are the same in week 12 as in week 1. That's what makes the trend real.

What Halsey does alone, and what needs me

An assistant that asks permission for everything is useless, and one that asks for nothing is dangerous. The design calls for Halsey's authority to be written down in a short charter that I approve, with scripts to enforce it. It isn't meant to be just a prompt it could talk itself out of.

On its ownAfter one approvalAlways my callNever
Read every agent's report · run the check-ins · write the daily summary and weekly review · keep the records log · research within set limits · make reports from existing templates · propose changes, including challenges Commission a build I approved once, and handle every review round until it passes or hits the limit · send a request that changes how an agent works Landing finished work · changing goals, milestones, values, or the charter · answering any decision Build software itself · publish anything · contact anyone or send messages as me · decide my values for me · edit another agent's files · change its own guardrails without me

The second column is the interesting one. Approving an idea once is enough for Halsey to see it through, including sending the work back for fixes, without coming back to me for every round. But it can never land the result without me. And Halsey can't change its own checks: its code and checkers are installed where it can only read them, and changes arrive only through reviewed builds I approve.

Getting things built without building them

Suppose Halsey notices that my study hours keep slipping and proposes a small chart of the trend on my goals card. I approve the idea once. From there:

  1. Halsey writes a commission for the builder: the goal it serves, the scope, what must not be touched, and a checklist of what "done" means for my goal, not just for the code. One checklist item might be: "a week with no answers shows as not reported, not as zero."
  2. The builder builds it in an isolated copy, where automated review and tests check the code.
  3. When the builder says it's ready, Halsey checks the result against the checklist, with evidence for each item, including screenshots.
  4. If it falls short, Halsey sends back a specific revision note ("item 2 failed: the week of the 7th is drawn as zero") for another round. There are three rounds by default.
  5. Only when every must-have passes do I see it: "ready for your look", with the review attached.

Halsey also knows when to stop. If the round limit is reached, the same item fails twice in a row, or a question comes up that only I can answer, it escalates. It tells me what passed, what failed, what was tried, and what my options are, instead of looping forever. The design calls for two independent reviews before anything reaches me: one on the code, and one against the goal.

Getting better on purpose

Every assistant hits things it can't do. The usual failure is to improvise quietly or fail silently. The plan has Halsey log a capability gap instead, with evidence: what it tried to do, what was missing (a skill, a script, some data, or some access), and which goal it affected.

It proposes a fix only when the gap is worth it. Either the gap blocks a goal's number outright, or the same gap has come up three or more times in four weeks. Otherwise it keeps watching, and a gap that doesn't recur fades away. When it does propose, it proposes the smallest fix: a new check-in question beats a new script, and a written procedure beats new code. A knowledge gap, like having no playbook for salary negotiation, becomes a research run rather than a build.

The throwaway scripts feed this too. If Halsey writes the same one-off calculation for the third time in a month, that's a repeated need. It proposes a proper, tested version, and that goes through the builder like any other build. Access gaps, like wanting to read my calendar, are never self-granted. They're always my decision, with a note on what data would flow where.

Memory that stays small

Long-running assistants tend to drown in their own notes. The planned memory has three tiers:

  • a small hot set, loaded every session under a fixed size budget: its rules, my goals, pinned facts, and a digest of today;
  • a warm set it loads only when a task needs it: recent reviews, the decision log, playbooks;
  • a searchable cold archive.

Entries are marked as pinned, aging, or perishable, and a weekly pass re-confirms or archives stale ones. Nothing unique is deleted; it's archived with its source. Halsey proposes things to remember ("studies best in the morning?") rather than saving them silently. The decision log is its institutional memory: every decision I make, in my own words, with the date and what it changed.

A week with Halsey, end to end

Here's how the pieces are meant to fit together over one ordinary week. The details are illustrative.

Monday. The 9:00 check-in picks up that I spent 45 minutes on the job search on Sunday and that I applied to a role on a company's own website. The application goes into the records log with a one-line read-back. Halsey's one topic for the day is the reply rate, which has been flat for three weeks. It's a short conversation, and it ends with a proposal to tailor two applications on Saturday mornings. At 13:00 there's nothing new. At 17:00 I mention that a recruiter reached out, and that goes into the log too.

Tuesday. I decline the 9:00 check-in because I'm in a meeting. Its two number questions wait on the daily page, and I tap the answers in at lunch. The 21:00 wrap-up takes four minutes: what got done, what didn't, and tomorrow's top three.

Wednesday. The job agent reports an application I'd already mentioned in a check-in. Halsey spots the probable match and asks whether it's the same one. It is, so it stays one entry with two sources. Meanwhile, a quick thesis-first research run on "does tailoring actually move reply rates?" comes back with one confirmed claim and two unverified ones. Halsey says exactly that, rather than rounding it up to "research shows".

Thursday. My study hours are behind for the week. At 17:00 Halsey doesn't nag. It asks whether the Thursday evening study block is still realistic, or whether to move it to the weekend and accept a lighter week.

Friday. A build I approved last week, a small trend chart for study hours, comes back from the builder. Halsey's review finds that one checklist item failed, because a week with no answers was drawn as zero, and sends it back with the exact failure. The second round passes, and it shows up on the daily page as "ready for your look".

Sunday. The weekly review: a scorecard with the same columns as every week, Halsey's read on what moved, two proposed changes and one challenge, a check-up on the tailoring experiment, and one question about whether a side project still fits what I said I care about. The records log answers "what did I actually do this week?" in one query. I accept one proposal, decline another, and answer the question in my own words. That answer goes into the decision log, so Halsey won't ask it again unless something changes.

Where the data goes

The design keeps Halsey's records on my own laptop. Goals, values, the records log, and metrics are meant to live in local files and small local databases, with no cloud service in between. The conversation command already uses an AI model; the planned features would also send some information outside the machine:

  • Anything I say in a check-in goes to the AI model that runs the conversation, and so does every records-log entry as it's captured. That includes things like how I applied for a role, or a referrer's name.
  • Because goals and values are now a continuous job, my values reach the model during check-ins, not just occasionally.
  • Nothing personal ever goes into a web search. Research questions are written in generic terms and checked before they run.
  • Throwaway scripts run in a sandbox with no network access and no access to my private folders.

How it's made

Four deploys, each one usable

Rather than building a large skeleton and hoping it all works at the end, the plan brings Halsey in four deploys. I'll use and test each one before the next starts.

The first deploy is deliberately modest. Its target is a Halsey I can talk with, a records log, and a daily page. Conversation, setup, and core safety are already merged; the records log is in development, and the daily page is still ahead. If it doesn't feel like a chief of staff when that deploy is complete, nothing after it will fix that.

Three layers of testing

  1. Automated tests on every change, using stand-ins for the AI and for the other agents. They're fast, free, and give the same answer every time, so a failure means the code changed, not the model's mood. They never touch anything real.
  2. Live tests before each deploy, with the real model and a throwaway copy of the other agents' mailboxes. These catch what stand-ins can't. Real answers drift from the expected shape, usage limits get hit, and command-line tools change their flags. The model's command-line tool went through several versions in the three days I spent designing this.
  3. My own product tests. This is the layer I care most about. I've picked a fixed set of real goal scenarios: an open-ended one (landing a new role) and a structured one (passing a certification exam). I'll run them through Halsey at every deploy with the same inputs, and score each answer on a rubric I wrote: specific, sourced, actionable, and fits my values. Since AI answers vary, each scenario runs twice. Comparing the scores deploy to deploy is how I'll know whether Halsey is actually getting better, and it's what I'll write about here.

On top of that, Halsey's own code goes through automated code review before it lands. As the later capabilities are built, their outputs will go through the checkers described above: the report structure check and the research re-check.

Why Python

Halsey is written in Python using only the standard library. Almost everything it does is data work: a small database, structured files, spreadsheets, a local web page, dates, and some statistics. Python's standard library covers all of that without installing anything, and it's much easier to test than shell scripts. The one constraint is that the Python that ships with my laptop is an older version, 3.9. The code has to stick to what 3.9 supports, which rules out a few newer language features.

What designing it taught me

  • Narrow interfaces beat clever integration. The one report format between Halsey and every other agent is the most important decision in the design. It's what makes it possible to add an agent without touching Halsey, or change an agent without breaking it.
  • "Not reported" is a feature. Drawing a missing week as zero is the most common way a personal dashboard lies. Refusing to guess, all the way through to the charts, keeps the numbers trustworthy.
  • Dull scripts beat clever prompts for anything that must be true. The design puts the research re-check, the report structure, the no-double-counting rule, and the authority limits in plain code with plain tests. The model does the thinking; the scripts will decide what's allowed and what's verified.
  • Conversation is expensive, so it has to earn its place. Moving from three taps to four conversations a day could multiply the time and AI cost many times over. The five-question cap and the twenty-second "nothing new" target are what keep it honest.
  • A chief of staff's most useful question isn't "did you do it?" It's "is this still the right thing to do?" That's why goals and values became a continuous job instead of a quarterly checkbox.

What's next

The design is done, and the first deploy is underway. I can already talk with Halsey and set it up. Next come the records log, a read-only view of what Halsey knows, a daily page, and the remaining installation work. Then I'll live with the complete first deploy for a couple of weeks and see whether the daily page earns its place. After that come the check-ins, which are the part I'm most curious and most skeptical about, followed by the thinking layer and the building layer.

Every design decision is now made and written down with what would make it worth revisiting, like whether four check-ins a day are worth the time. I'll write up the product-test scores after each deploy, including the ones that go badly.
